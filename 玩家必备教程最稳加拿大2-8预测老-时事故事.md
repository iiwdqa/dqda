Re-elected Kelowna Mayor Tom Dyas is promising better communication and more listening as the city’s new council begins its term.

But whether that includes greater access to individual councillors before decisions are made remains an open question.

At Monday’s swearing-in ceremony, Dyas pledged to find more ways to listen and communicate. Asked what that would look like, he acknowledged there is room for improvement.

“Over the last few months, we’ve heard that there needs to be some improvement with regards to communications,” Dyas said, adding that the city is looking at what changes could be made.

One issue that has drawn scrutiny is councillors’ reluctance to speak publicly to the media about matters coming before council.

During Dyas’ first term, journalists were often told the city’s code of conduct prevented councillors from commenting on issues before they were debated at the council table.

However, the code does not explicitly prohibit elected officials from expressing their personal opinions on municipal issues before or after council considers them.

Dyas maintains that councillors should wait until they have received all the information presented during a council meeting before communicating their position on a matter.

“To speak to an item before a decision is made at a council meeting would not be appropriate,” he said, explaining that information presented during deliberations could change a councillor’s thinking.

Dyas argues the approach allows councillors to make informed decisions without outside discussion influencing the process.

But Sean Holman, a journalism professor at the University of Victoria and the Wayne Crooks Professor in Environmental and Climate Journalism, says limiting public discussion until after decisions are made raises concerns about transparency and accountability.

“The only thing that matters is the decision after the fact and that’s just wrong,” Holman said. “We should be able to know why government is making the decisions that it makes.”

Holman says increasingly scripted communications and restrictions on access to elected officials are eroding the public’s right to know and undermining trust in government.

“What Canadians want more than anything else from their elected officials is authenticity and the truth,” he said.

Some of the city’s newest councillors say they intend to take a more open approach.

Newly-elected councillor Les Bellamy says he has no intention of being silenced and believes the code of conduct does not prevent councillors from speaking about general municipal matters.

“I think councils may have been encouraged not to speak to matters and I will do so respectfully,” Bellamy said. “The code of conduct does not limit what I can say.”

Bellamy says he wants to ensure he has all the relevant facts before offering an opinion, but also wants to see more opportunities for residents to have their say before decisions are made.

He intends to bring forward a motion asking city staff to investigate ways to improve direct communication between the community and council, potentially through dedicated public input time at meetings or townhalls.

Fellow newcomer Kevin Kraft also says he is comfortable speaking with the media before council meetings.

“I’m fine being accessible even prior to a meeting,” Kraft said. “With five new councillors joining the eight-member council, whether a change in council brings a change in communication remains to be seen.”

What appeared to be a neck-and-neck race between two top contenders for the mayor’s seat helped draw more people to the polls in Toronto, leading to the city’s highest turnout in more than a decade, some experts say.

Nearly one million people in the city cast a ballot in this week’s municipal election, and unofficial results collected by the Association of Municipalities of Ontario show 46 per cent of those eligible to vote in Toronto did so.

Meanwhile, the average turnout across the province was around 36 per cent, according to the association’s data.

Some political scientists say a competitive race, increased civic engagement, a stronger political culture and greater media coverage all contributed to the higher turnout in Toronto.

The top driver for Toronto’s increased voter participation was the apparent close contest between mayoral front-runners Olivia Chow and Brad Bradford, as well as tight races for council positions in several wards, said Cristine de Clercy, a political scientist who teaches at Trent University.

“It attracted attention, and it seems to me an important effect that that contest, which seemed to become closer as the campaign wore on … certainly had a large effect on turnout.”

Polls placing the two so close, including one that put Bradford ahead just days before the election, motivated voters and campaign volunteers because they felt their efforts and votes could make a difference, she said.

“So that certainly got some other people out to the polls who normally might not have voted if they thought either the incumbent was going to win in a landslide, or her opponent was going to win a landslide,” she said.

Community organizations also played an important role in getting people to vote in Toronto during this municipal election cycle, she said.

While the competitive race between Chow and Bradford was the main draw, Myer Siemiatycki, a professor emeritus of politics and public administration at Toronto Metropolitan University, said that alone couldn’t explain why Toronto’s turnout eclipsed that of other municipalities with hard-fought elections.

In nearby Mississauga, where both the incumbent Carolyn Parrish and her predecessor Bonnie Crombie lost a close three-way race to Alvin Tedjo, 34 per cent of eligible voters cast ballots. While that represents a 12-point surge from 2022, it’s still significantly lower than in Toronto, Siemiatycki said.

“I think the difference in Toronto is number one, there’s a much stronger political culture of participation in the city than in Mississauga,” which many still consider a suburb, he said. “Generally, central cities have a stronger history and pattern of political participation than suburban areas do.”

Robust media coverage in Toronto, along with a wide range of issues that people cared about, such as bike lanes, school nutrition programs and traffic gridlock, could also have fuelled higher turnout, he said.

Chow’s army of volunteers also made a difference, he said.

“She has a lot of very dedicated supporters who, on election day, canvass and knock on doors and encourage people to go out and vote, and that certainly played out to drive up the vote,” he said.

Meanwhile, the declining media landscape in smaller communities has created an information gap, Siemiatycki said.

“Whereas in Toronto, of course, there’s such a rich and multifaceted, diverse media ecosystem, there’s just a lot more information, a lot of reminders and focus on the municipal election race,” he said.

Despite being higher than the rest of the province, the turnout in Toronto is “still too low and is a problem and we need to think about how to increase it,” he added.

The Association of Municipalities of Ontario’s data show more than four million Ontarians voted across the province, including Toronto, where the city said 989,990 ballots were cast. Chow secured victory with 532,201 certified votes compared to Bradford’s 404,013.

In London, Ont., where the incumbent Josh Morgan was re-elected, the turnout was 31 per cent, an increase of around six percentage points from 2022. Morgan got over 45 per cent of the total votes, while his closest competitor received a little over 26 per cent.

Cameron D. Anderson, a professor of political science at the University of Western Ontario, said the city’s voter turnout has historically been behind Toronto’s, but the lack of tough opposition for Morgan further dampened participation.

“Generally speaking … it was assumed that he would win again,” Anderson said. “Whereas in Chow’s case, there was a sense that, oh no, look, the challenger is getting really, really close and maybe could beat her,” which increases the motivation to vote, he said.

The Association of Municipalities of Ontario said unofficial results show that voter turnout varied considerably across municipalities with populations of over 100,000.

Chatham-Kent and Greater Sudbury, for example, were at 41 per cent while Kitchener had the lowest turnout at 22 per cent.
